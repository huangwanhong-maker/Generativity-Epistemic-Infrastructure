# Cross-Domain Research, Standards, and Institutional Programme

**Document class:** Informative programme planning  
**Status:** PROVISIONAL development proposal; no adoption or institutional endorsement  
**Version:** 0.1  
**Date and source check:** 2026-09-22, Asia/Tokyo (2026-09-21 UTC)  
**Programme context:** [Comprehensive development plan](comprehensive_development_plan.md)

## 1. Purpose and development approach

The programme's scope includes the conditions under which people recognize reality, assess evidence, make judgments, act together, and preserve or reopen possibilities. A comprehensive development programme therefore needs substantive work in epistemology, jurisprudence, ethics, governance, political economy, public policy, archival practice, and technical design. These domains contribute objectives and constraints, not merely metadata fields for an already selected ontology.

The proposed organizing question is: **what practices and institutions make a factual or practical judgment inspectable, contestable, revisable, and appropriately actionable, under unequal conditions of knowledge and power?** Technical recording is one part of the response. Better records alone do not establish warranted beliefs, legitimate authority, justice, or beneficial outcomes.

This plan extends the [multilayer architecture](../framework/multilayer_architecture.md). It uses existing GR families; domain headings are work areas, not new publication numbers or ontology classes. Standards-track work identifies the normative problem first. Specifications follow demonstrated infrastructure needs. Technical Reports develop unresolved concepts and comparisons; manuals support practice; international policy proposals compare institutional choices. No draft is an adopted Standard.

All programme actions below are **GR operational proposals** unless explicitly described as source-derived. Foundation manuscripts remain the conceptual sources for GR terms. External instruments offer questions and constraints for review; they do not establish equivalence with GR or automatically become programme requirements.

## 3. Substantive development domains

**Structural role:** give each domain its own questions, outputs, dependencies, reviewers, and evidence of practical usefulness. Proposed roles are unassigned.

### 3.1 Epistemology and inquiry methodology

**Objective and questions:** develop practices of warranted recognition, not only labels for confidence. What differentiates observation, testimony, trace, inference, interpretation, and institutional acceptance? How do methods establish their scope and limitations? How are counterevidence, absent evidence, conflicting accounts, and changing categories handled? When is judgment sufficiently supported for a specified action, while remaining revisable?

**Outputs and dependencies:** GR-100/200/210 standards-track requirements; source analysis in GR-TR101; a methods/assessment manual contribution; GR-SPEC-200 only for reusable representation and interaction needs. Depend on foundation interpretation, domain methods, presentation design, and institutional decision contexts. Keep empirical, interpretive, and normative warrant distinguishable rather than imposing one confidence scale.

**Review and validation:** epistemologists, qualitative and quantitative researchers, domain practitioners, and affected knowledge holders examine an inquiry with competing explanations. Test whether an independent reviewer can reconstruct the grounds, limits, and reasons for changed judgment, including whether important testimony was excluded by the recording categories.

### 3.2 Ontology, concepts, and formal semantics

**Objective and questions:** establish enough conceptual precision for interoperability while exposing the limits of operational categories. What makes a recorded subject persist? How do event, relation, and record identity differ? What changes when an institution changes its classification? Which theoretical distinctions are lost in a proposed formalization?

**Outputs and dependencies:** GR-100/120 drafts, GR-TR101 interpretation, GR-TR102 experimental semantics, and bounded GR-SPEC-120 models. Definitions remain grounded in manuscripts; unresolved formulations are preserved. Formalism is evaluated against epistemic, archival, and institutional cases rather than deciding their ontology in advance.

**Review and validation:** GR scholars, philosophers, formal-methods researchers, and implementers compare ordinary, disputed, and counterexample cases. Evidence includes explicit assumptions, mapping limits, and failures of equivalence. Category-theoretic work remains exploratory until it demonstrates an operational need; no mathematical representation is treated as proof of empirical or institutional legitimacy.

### 3.3 Archival practice and information science

**Objective and questions:** preserve interpretable evidence and trajectories through changing custody, formats, and descriptions. What is selected, excluded, appraised, or lost? Whose history survives? How are restricted evidence, custodial changes, retrospective description, and documented destruction handled without suggesting completeness?

**Outputs and dependencies:** GR-110/140/160 requirements, GR-M101 archival guidance, and GR-SPEC-120/130 packaging and migration details when needed. Compare existing archival approaches through the technical landscape. Depend on legal retention context, ethical access decisions, temporal semantics, and resource limits.

**Review and validation:** archivists, records managers, information scientists, community archives, and access specialists test an incomplete collection and a migration. Evaluate interpretability, recoverable relationships, disclosed losses, selective access, and maintenance burden. Preservation quality is not measured simply by the volume retained or made public.

### 3.4 Jurisprudence, legal recognition, and procedure

**Objective and questions:** investigate how legal systems recognize records, persons, contributions, authority, and procedural acts. Separate authenticity, reliability, admissibility, evidentiary weight, and a competent body's finding. Examine notice, reply, correction, restriction, confidentiality, privilege, retention, and review without assuming identical rules across institutions or jurisdictions.

**Outputs and dependencies:** informative comparative legal analysis first; scoped GR-300/310 profiles after review; interfaces to GR-160/210/400. Each proposal identifies the legal concept, forum, relevant actors, legal source and edition, asserted application, procedural consequences, and unresolved conflicts. A data field saying “right,” “consent,” or “binding” is a recorded assertion, not a legal conclusion.

**Review and validation:** relevant jurisdictional counsel, evidence/procedure scholars, institutional legal officers, and affected-party representatives examine a synthetic dispute before real deployment. Test whether readers distinguish a party's legal claim, an institutional policy, a pending proceeding, and an operative decision. Use the profile worksheet in section 4; no generic conformance result substitutes for legal analysis.

### 3.5 Ethics, value pluralism, and justice

**Objective and questions:** examine who benefits, who bears costs, who is heard, and which relationships or future possibilities are damaged. Include distributive justice, recognition, epistemic exclusion, autonomy, care, non-domination, ecological effects, and intergenerational consequences. How are conflicting values represented and reasons for prioritization made answerable to affected people?

**Outputs and dependencies:** an ethical-assessment Technical Report and deliberation guidance; candidate process requirements within GR-110/140/160/210/400 where testable. Cross-reference UNESCO's AI ethics and open-science recommendations within their scope. Keep descriptive consequences, value commitments, and proposed remedies distinct. No universal ethical ranking or generativity score is proposed.

**Review and validation:** ethicists, disability/access specialists, labor and community representatives, and people bearing the proposed practice's costs examine alternative designs. Test inclusion of dissent, unjustified credibility discounts, unequal recording burdens, coercive visibility, and meaningful refusal. Evaluate whether review changes decisions and remedies harms, rather than treating completion of a checklist as evidence of ethical adequacy.

### 3.6 Institutional and public governance

**Objective and questions:** specify accountable uses of evidence in decision-making. Who sets categories, appoints assessors, controls access, can contest a finding, and provides remedy? How are conflicts of interest, unequal participation, institutional capture, urgent decisions, and failures to act exposed? Distinguish governance of this standards programme from governance of an institution using its outputs.

**Outputs and dependencies:** programme governance procedures; GR-400 organizational requirements; GR-210/310 review interfaces; decision-record and oversight manuals. OECD evidence-governance analysis motivates examining the conditions of evidence use alongside evidence quality. Depend on legitimate mandates, resourced review roles, ethics, and practical access.

**Review and validation:** public-administration specialists, institutional practitioners, civil-society participants, and independent oversight reviewers conduct a contested decision exercise. Assess who can trigger review, whether reasons address the challenge, whether remedial action occurs, and whether downstream effects are examined. More documentation alone is not evidence of accountability.

### 3.7 Political economy, contribution, and generativity return

**Objective and questions:** investigate ownership, infrastructural control, incentives, labor, access, and distribution of benefits. Who funds recording and preservation? Who can extract value, control classifications, or exclude contributors? How do documentation requirements alter bargaining power? Which maintenance, care, community, or environmental contributions remain invisible?

**Outputs and dependencies:** source-grounded research on contribution and generativity return; comparative institutional/economic Technical Reports; GR-110/160/400 candidate requirements for disclosure and contestable attribution; separate policy options for funding and stewardship. Generativity return remains a theoretical and operational research question, not an assumed monetary amount, entitlement, or contractual distribution rule.

**Review and validation:** political economists, labor researchers, infrastructure operators, community stewards, and contributors compare centralized custody, distributed stewardship, and existing repositories. Record cost allocation, exit/migration capacity, uncompensated work, access barriers, and concentration of control. A policy decision about benefit-sharing remains distinguishable from an empirical claim about contribution.

### 3.8 Public policy and international institutional options

**Objective and questions:** determine whether a public intervention is justified, for whom, under which mandate, and with what alternatives. Separate evidence about a problem and expected effects from political choices about priorities, funding, authority, participation, and acceptable risk.

**Outputs and dependencies:** mandate-specific proposals in the [international-organizations track](../../policy_proposals/international_organizations/README.md), using its policy template; options appraisal, theory of change, capacity-building plan, and evaluation design. Standards supply referenced technical requirements only after adoption; pilot Specifications are explicitly identified as experimental. Section 5 supplies initial options.

**Review and validation:** policy analysts, relevant institutional mandate/legal specialists, development and capacity-building practitioners, affected communities, and evaluators compare benefits, costs, alternatives, failure conditions, and exit paths. No organization is presumed to sponsor or participate; real pilots follow its own authorization and governance.

### 3.9 Technical implementation, security, usability, and accessibility

**Objective and questions:** implement the agreed practices without losing semantic distinctions or excluding participants. Can people inspect, challenge, correct, restrict, and move their records? What happens under forgery, coercive access, link failure, malicious submissions, conflicting updates, or inaccessible interfaces? Which tasks remain feasible offline or with limited resources?

**Outputs and dependencies:** GR-120/130/140/150/160 drafts, GR-SPEC-120/130/200, protocol behavior, conformance evidence, and user guidance. Use the [protocol plan](protocol_specification_plan.md) and verified external candidates. Security mechanisms follow threat analysis; accessibility assessment includes assistive technology, language, comprehension, bandwidth, and supported human alternatives.

**Review and validation:** independent implementers, security/privacy specialists, interaction designers, accessibility practitioners, and users test positive and failure cases. Combine semantic exchange tests with task-based comprehension and contestation exercises. Technical validation, source authentication, evidential assessment, ethical judgment, and legal status receive separate results.

## 4. Legal and institutional profile worksheet

**Structural role:** make proposed legal interfaces reviewable without universalizing a local conclusion.

| Element | Information to establish for a concrete profile |
|---|---|
| Concept and action | For example electronic writing, evidentiary weight, notice, confidentiality, access, correction, preservation, or remedy; precise operation affected |
| Context and competence | Named jurisdiction, institution/forum, actors, territorial and temporal scope, and decision-maker's asserted authority |
| Basis and status | Exact instrument/provision/version; treaty, enacted rule, case, institutional policy, model rule, or proposal; application and interpretation verified separately |
| Evidence and procedure | Material relied on, evidentiary assessment method, ability to respond, relevant deadlines, notice route, review/appeal and remedies |
| Tensions and exclusions | Conflicting duties, confidentiality versus disclosure, preservation versus disposal, cross-border issues, unresolved authority, and unavailable evidence |
| Technical implication | Limited record/protocol behavior implementing the reviewed profile; distinctions and assumptions retained in exchange and presentation |

The initial output is a worksheet completed for a synthetic institution, with legal applicability left OPEN. A live profile needs named expertise and current primary legal sources. Treaty guarantees are not mechanically converted into software permissions, and UNCITRAL's commercial-law model is not presumed applicable to noncommercial archives or public decision processes.

## 5. International policy options and institutional fit

**Structural role:** compare institutional arrangements rather than presenting technical adoption as an inevitable political choice. The audiences below are possible recipients, not partners or endorsers.

| Possible audience and relevant remit | Candidate proposal | Mandate boundary to verify |
|---|---|---|
| UNESCO-related science, education, culture, and information communities | Voluntary research/knowledge-preservation pilots, inclusive participation, and capacity-building, informed by the two recommendations above | Specific programme authority, funding, participant protection, and relation to existing open-science infrastructure |
| OECD public-governance and policy-evaluation communities | Comparative study of evidence-to-decision practices and organizational capacities | Participation arrangements and policy-learning remit; no assumption of legal authority over national decisions |
| Institutions considering electronic-record legislative reform, informed by UNCITRAL work | A bounded comparison of technical record functions with locally relevant legal requirements | Commercial scope, actual enactment, competent lawmakers and courts; no claim that GR itself supplies a model law |
| Human-rights and oversight bodies within their respective mandates | Review of participation, unequal recognition, disclosure, and remedies in proposed pilots | Competence, independence, procedural basis, and protection of affected people; no assumed infrastructure-operating mandate |

Every policy proposal compares at least these plausible options where relevant: improve practices using **existing infrastructure**; run a **voluntary bounded pilot** with reversible commitments; develop **shared stewardship** with explicit representation, funding, custody, and exit arrangements; or **defer adoption** while improving evidence, capacity, or safeguards. A broader rollout depends on demonstrated benefits, burdens, and institutional authority.

Appraisal records the baseline, intended beneficiaries, theory of change, costs and opportunity costs, distributional effects, implementation capacity, procurement/control arrangements, safeguards, independent evaluation, and conditions for stopping or revising the intervention. Empirical findings inform these choices; they do not resolve value conflicts or determine the politically legitimate option by themselves.

## 6. Worked case: contested research-funding decision

**Structural role:** demonstrate cross-domain distinctions in one synthetic example, without alleging facts or legal duties about a real institution.

A fictional public research fund rejects a community-led project after an AI-assisted summary describes its water-monitoring evidence as unreliable. A reviewer had treated missing instrument-calibration metadata as evidence that no calibration occurred. Community participants provide a restricted maintenance log and oral testimony. They also challenge the funding criteria's treatment of local knowledge. A reconsideration panel later changes its assessment; a small contributor's work still lacks recognition.

| Layer | Distinct account and proposed recording/review question |
|---|---|
| Situation and trajectory | Equipment use, measurements, collaboration, rejection, and later reconsideration are subjects of inquiry. What records support each account, and what capacities were interrupted by the delay? |
| Evidence and interpretation | The initial reviewer inferred unreliability from missing metadata; the log and testimony offer counterevidence. Record method, provenance, limitations, and rival explanations; neither a missing field nor a valid signature decides the factual question. |
| Categories and methodology | The fund's scoring rules privilege certain evidence formats. Examine their purpose, validity, and exclusions separately from whether the project satisfied them. A changed rule needs its own history. |
| Normative assessment | Participants argue that dismissal of local testimony was unjust and burdens were unequally allocated. Preserve their reasons and counterarguments; the record does not translate this judgment into a universal ethics score. |
| Legal/procedural position | A participant asserts a right to reconsideration. Record the asserted basis, relevant forum and unresolved applicability; a legal reviewer identifies actual rules before representing an entitlement or violation as determined. |
| Institutional decision | The panel records authority, recusals, evidence considered, reasons, notice, and disposition. A revised funding decision is distinct from accepting every factual or normative argument. |
| Political economy and return | Document uncompensated evidence-production labor, repository control, contribution disputes, and funding effects. Recognition, compensation, and future access are separate policy choices requiring reasons. |
| Preservation and implementation | Maintain authorized access to earlier assessments and the restricted log, disclose public-view limits, and exchange the revised account without erasing dissent or leaking contributors' details. |

Pilot evaluation asks whether a participant can understand and challenge the initial reasoning; an independent reviewer can recover the change; protected contributors remain protected; and the institution can demonstrate its response. Follow-up examines actual effects on research participation and capacity. It does not infer restored trust, justice, or generativity from a corrected database entry.

## 7. Cross-domain review and sequencing

**Structural role:** make interdisciplinary review part of development decisions, before institutional use and publication.

At requirements development, each work item identifies its conceptual, epistemic, ethical, legal, institutional, economic, archival, and implementation dependencies. Editors document alternatives and disagreements jointly; a technical convenience does not silently settle a dispute about rights, values, authority, or knowledge.

Before a real-data pilot, proposed readiness evidence includes a scoped purpose and institutional basis; affected-participant involvement; proportionate data/custody arrangements; ethics and rights review; accessible challenge and remedy routes; security assessment; resourced oversight; and explicit stop/revision conditions. Missing expertise leads to a narrower synthetic case or deferred live use, not an unsupported assurance claim.

Before public review or publication, each relevant domain supplies a review record with reviewer competence, conflicts of interest, examined cases, unresolved objections, and disposition. Technical exchange, evidential usefulness, procedural legitimacy, accessibility, burdens, distributional consequences, and preservation remain separately assessed. Significant cross-domain changes return to affected reviewers.

Immediate outputs are a domain responsibility/dependency map, the synthetic funding case, initial legal-profile and policy-options worksheets, and a common issue register. Subsequent work develops the smallest coordinated GR-100/110/140/150/160/200/210/400 requirements subset; scoped GR-300/310 legal profiles advance when a concrete context and expertise exist. New independent publications follow demonstrated needs rather than multiplying family numbers now.

**OPEN:** reviewer recruitment and representation; which institutional context to study first; appropriate evidence for practical benefits and burdens; contribution/return operationalization; differences among knowledge traditions and methods; and when unresolved value or legal conflicts require narrowed scope. Record dispositions in [open questions](open_questions.md) and architecture decisions where appropriate.
